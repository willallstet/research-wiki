# In Free Fall: A Thought Experiment on Vertical Perspective

**By:** Hito Steyerl · **Published:** *e-flux journal* #24 (April 2011) ·
[read the essay](https://www.e-flux.com/journal/24/67860/in-free-fall-a-thought-experiment-on-vertical-perspective)

## What it argues

Steyerl's thought experiment: contemporary visual culture is **"in free fall."**
The stable **horizon line** that underwrote linear perspective — and with it a
stable observer, calculable space, and linear progressive time — has given way to
a groundless condition. "Imagine you are falling. But there is no ground … the
present moment is distinguished by a prevailing condition of groundlessness"
(essay §opening). Falling is *relational*: "if there is nothing to fall toward,
you may not even be aware that you're falling," and it "may actually feel like
perfect stasis — as if history and time have ended" (§opening). The loss of the
horizon is the loss of "a stable paradigm of orientation, which has situated
concepts of subject and object, of time and space, throughout modernity."

## A brief history of the horizon

The horizon was a navigational instrument — Arab navigators sighting Polaris
against it with fingers or an arrow held at arm's length, then the astrolabe,
quadrant, and sextant (§"A Brief History of the Horizon"). Crucially, "the ground
on which sailors stood was never stable in the first place. The stable horizon
mostly remained a projection, until artificial horizons were eventually invented
in order to create the illusion of stability." Navigation "gave seafarers a sense
of orientation, thus also enabling colonialism and the spread of a capitalist
global market," and grounded the optical paradigm of modernity: **linear
perspective**. Steyerl traces it from Alhazen's *Kitab al-Manazir* (1028) through
Duccio's still-multiple vanishing points to Uccello's single vanishing point on a
virtual horizon.

## Perspective as abstraction and as domination

Following Panofsky, Steyerl argues linear perspective "declares the view of a
one-eyed and immobile spectator as a norm — and this view is itself assumed to be
natural, scientific, and objective," when in fact it "is based on an abstraction,
and does not correspond to any subjective perception." It computes "a
mathematical, flattened, infinite, continuous, and homogenous space, and declares
it to be reality" — a *perspectiva*, a "seeing through," yielding a space that is
"calculable, navigable, and predictable." Citing Benjamin, she notes "time can
become just as homogenous and empty as space"
([[benjamin-work-of-art-mechanical-reproduction]] is the source here). Perspective
both empowers the viewer (mirrored in the vanishing point) and subjects them to
"supposedly objective laws." Her case study is Uccello's *Miracle of the
Desecrated Host* (1465–69), whose converging parallels frame a Jewish merchant at
the stake — "linear perspective becomes a matrix for racial and religious
propaganda," prefiguring the 1492 expulsions and Columbus's expedition.

## The downfall — and the vertical turn

Perspective "carries the seeds of its own downfall": its universal claim to truth
made it "a hostage to the truth it had so confidently proclaimed." Steyerl reads
Turner's *The Slave Ship* (1840) — slaves thrown overboard for insurance — as the
horizon "tilted, curved, and troubled," the observer's stable position abandoned;
and *Rain, Steam, and Speed* (1844), painted after Turner stuck his head out of a
moving train, as perspective dissolving. Cubism, montage, relativity, and aviation
continue the dismantling. Then the **vertical/aerial** paradigm arrives: Google
Maps, satellite, drone, and 3D cinema stage "vertiginous flights into abysses."
Drawing on Elsaesser's "military-surveillance-entertainment complex," Eyal
Weizman's **vertical 3D sovereignty** (space split into stacked layers, his
example the Israeli occupation of Palestine), and Achille Mbembe's **necropolitics**
("Occupation of the skies … most of the policing is done from the air" — UAVs,
reconnaissance jets, Hawkeyes, satellites, "hologrammatization").

## The key reversal: the aerial view fakes its ground

Steyerl's sharpest move: these aerial views "do not actually portray a stable
ground. Instead, they create a supposition that it exists in the first place …
Retroactively, this virtual ground creates a perspective of overview and
surveillance for a distanced, superior spectator safely floating up in the air."
This is "a radicalization — though not an overcoming — of the paradigm of linear
perspective": the subject/object split "turned into the one-way gaze of superiors
onto inferiors," a "disembodied and remote-controlled gaze, outsourced to machines
and other objects."

## Free fall as terror and freedom

Via Adorno's *Negative Dialectics* — who "scoffs at philosophy's obsession with
earth and origin" and calls the vertigo of throwing oneself toward objects an
"index veri" — Steyerl reframes groundlessness. "What seemed like a helpless
tumble into an abyss actually turns out to be a new representational freedom …
perhaps this helps us get over the last assumption … the idea that we need a
ground in the first place." Free fall is both "a social and political dreamscape
of radicalized class war from above" and "a freedom that is terrifying, utterly
deterritorializing." "But falling does not only mean falling apart, it can also
mean a new certainty falling into place … no community, but a shifting formation."

## Assessment

- **A primary artifact, read on its own terms.** This is Steyerl's canonical essay
  and a touchstone of post-2010 image theory. Its worth here is as the source text
  the wiki's Steyerl cluster refers back to, judged by the force of its argument
  rather than by empirical fact-checking.
- **Standing on citations that have rotted.** Companion crawl PR #9 found that
  three of the essay's load-bearing footnotes (Elsaesser's *Return of 3-D*, which
  Steyerl calls the paper's "blueprint"; Weizman's *Politics of Verticality*;
  Gil Leung's response) have link-rotted. Any concept page built on *vertical
  perspective / groundlessness* should pin to archived copies rather than the live
  e-flux footnote links.
