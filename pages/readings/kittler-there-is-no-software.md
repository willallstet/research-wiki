# There Is No Software

**By:** Friedrich Kittler · **Published:** 1992 (this text: reprinted in
*Literature Media Information Systems*, ed. John Johnston, 1997; pp. 147–155) ·
read in full from the local PDF.

A foundational media-theory reading tracked for [[media-infrastructure]] (the
priority of hardware over software; the physical substrate the software layer
hides) and [[ai-and-machine-vision]] (the computational metaphor, the
Church–Turing hypothesis, and the limits of digitizing "nature"). Kittler is a
touchstone for the wiki's hardware-substrate thread and a direct ancestor of
[[sea-monsters-as-medium]]'s "physical internet" argument. Read in full; the
scanned PDF is OCR-noisy, so quotations are lightly normalized and cited to the
printed page numbers (147–155).

## What it argues

Kittler's provocation is in the title: **there is no software** — or rather, there
*would* be none "if computer systems were not surrounded by an environment of
everyday languages" (`…pdf:150`). Software is a commercially and legally useful
fiction layered over what is, in the last analysis, "signals of voltage
differences" (`…pdf:150`). The essay's real claim is the **priority of hardware**:
"there are good grounds to assume the indispensability and, consequently, the
priority of hardware in general" (`…pdf:152`).

## The argument

- **Writing has become invisible, and self-executing.** The bulk of written text
  "no longer exist[s] in perceivable time and space, but in a computer memory's
  transistor cells" (`…pdf:147`). Unlike all historical writing tools, these
  microscopic inscriptions "are able to read and write by themselves"
  (`…pdf:147`). Kittler dates "the last historical act of writing" to the early
  1970s, when Intel engineers hand-laid the blueprint of the 4004 microprocessor
  before it was miniaturized and "written into silicon layers" (`…pdf:147`) — after
  which Computer-Aided Design let each chip generation lay out the next.

- **The software tower hides its own hardware.** From opcodes up through
  assemblers to high-level languages, "far-reaching chains of self-similarities in
  the sense defined by fractal theory organize the software as well as the
  hardware of every writing" — layers "explicitly contrived to evade perception.
  We simply do not know what our writing does" (`…pdf:148`). His running example is
  DOS/WordPerfect: the grandiose name *WordPerfect* is advertising; the eight-letter
  acronym `WP` is what the operating system can actually read, and "WP does what it
  says" (`…pdf:149`). Each apparent agent (WP) only runs "under DOS," which resolves
  into BIOS, which resolves into functions "burnt into silicon and thus form part
  of the hardware," requiring "a material event called reset" to start at all
  (`…pdf:149–150`).

- **Formalization empties out meaning down to voltage.** Invoking Hilbert,
  Kittler notes that formalization "does away with theory itself": a theory becomes
  "sentences as sequences of words, which are in turn sequences of letters," until
  "when meanings come down to sentences, sentences to words, and words to letters,
  there is no software at all" (`…pdf:150`). The "philosophy of the computer
  community" systematically "obscure[s] hardware with software" (`…pdf:150`);
  GUIs "dispense with writing itself" and hide "a whole machine from its users,"
  and protection software walls off the kernel (`…pdf:150–151`).

- **Software's legal existence is a cryptographic trick.** By Turing's proof, "the
  concept of mental property as applied to algorithms has become meaningless," yet
  "precisely because software does not exist as a machine-independent faculty,
  software as a commercial or American medium insists on its status as property all
  the more" (`…pdf:151`). Licenses, dongles, and trademarks work "exactly the same
  way as one-way functions in recent mathematical cryptography" — hiding an
  algorithm from its result (`…pdf:151`). He cites IBM's **logical depth** (the
  value of a message as "the amount of mathematical or other work plausibly done by
  its originator") as a would-be rigorous replacement for authorship/copyright —
  except the algorithm to compute it "is Turing-uncomputable itself" (`…pdf:151–152`).
  German criminal law, he notes, has "abandoned the very concept of software as
  mental property; instead, it defines software as necessarily a material thing"
  (`…pdf:152`).

- **You cannot digitize nature.** Turing's unbounded paper tape exists only on
  paper; "all physically feasible machines … are limited by these parameters in
  their very code" (`…pdf:152`). Quoting Hasslacher (Los Alamos), computation
  "reduce[s] a continuous algorithmic description" to a discrete, binary form
  "determined largely by machine constraints" — yielding "a discrete and synthetic
  microworld image" arbitrarily fixed, "very far from Turing machines" (`…pdf:153`).
  The Church–Turing hypothesis, in its strong physical form, amounts to "declaring
  nature itself a universal Turing machine" — injecting "algorithmic behavior into
  the behavior of the physical world for which there is no evidence" (`…pdf:148,
  153`).

- **Programmability is a property of hardware, not software.** From Shannon's 1937
  proof that telegraph relays implement Boolean algebra, to the integrated circuit
  pairing silicon (a controllable resistor) with its own oxide (a near-perfect
  isolator), "the programmability of matter could finally 'take control'"
  (`…pdf:153`). "This all-important property of being programmable has, in all
  evidence, nothing to do with software; it is an exclusive feature of hardwares"
  (`…pdf:153`). Structural programmability requires "a base set of elementary-type
  components that can be reconfigured" — a combination lock is not; a digital
  computer is (`…pdf:154`).

- **The closing turn: noise, connectivity, and the limits of the discrete.** The
  price of composable discrete elements is a drawback in *connectivity* — computing
  power's growth is bounded by "a square root function," unable to "keep up with
  polynomial growth rates in problem size" (`…pdf:154`). "Nonprogrammable systems,
  be they waves or beings," have maximal connectivity and polynomial complexity;
  only "sheer hardware, a physical device working amidst physical devices," could
  match them (`…pdf:154`). Kittler ends by noting the silicon chip *already*
  seethes with un-programmed physics — "electron diffusion … quantum-mechanical
  tunneling all over the chip" — currently "treated in terms of system
  limitations, physical side effects." "To minimize all the noise" is "the price we
  pay for structurally programmable machines"; the "inverse strategy of maximizing
  noise … may well be the only way to enter that body of real numbers originally
  known as chaos" (`…pdf:154–155`).

## Why it's here

- **Concept fit:** [[media-infrastructure]] — this is the theoretical spine of the
  wiki's hardware-substrate thread. Kittler's "priority of hardware" and his
  account of how each software layer is "contrived to evade perception" is the
  direct ancestor of [[will-allstetter]]'s **infrastructure-made-visible** interest:
  the GPUHammer curse-tablet video (a bit-flip in silicon collapsing a model) is
  almost a Kittlerian parable, and the *Document Journal* cloud/data-center piece
  shares his impulse to drag the physical substrate back into view.
- **Concept fit:** [[ai-and-machine-vision]] — the essay is a precise critique of
  the **computational metaphor**: the Church–Turing hypothesis as an unwarranted
  claim about nature, and the impossibility of losslessly "digitalizing the body of
  real numbers formerly known as nature" (`…pdf:152`). This pairs directly with
  [[chirimuuta-reflex-machine-cybernetic-brain]]'s abstraction argument against
  computationalism — Kittler and Chirimuuta attack the "brain/nature is a computer"
  move from the hardware and the neuroscience sides respectively.
- **Lineage & orbit:** shares the media-materialist milieu of
  [[sea-monsters-as-medium]] (the physical internet as the real medium) and, from
  the opposite direction, complements [[benjamin-work-of-art-mechanical-reproduction]]
  — where Benjamin makes the reproduction *apparatus* the agent of perceptual
  change, Kittler makes the silicon substrate the agent that software conceals.

## Assessment

- **A primary artifact of media theory, read on its own terms.** Kittler's essay
  is a polemic, not an empirical study; its force is in the argument-image ("there
  is no software," writing that reads and writes itself) rather than in
  fact-checkable claims. Per the wiki's stance, it is judged as a reading whose
  worth is the provocation it stages, not its literal engineering accuracy — some of
  its technical particulars (DOS 8.3 filenames, the 4004 anecdote) are of their
  early-1990s moment.
- **A hub text for the infrastructure thread.** It supplies the canonical statement
  that the physical substrate has priority over the abstractions layered on it — the
  claim every other reading in [[media-infrastructure]] circles, and a useful
  counterweight to any wiki page that treats "software," "the model," or "the cloud"
  as if it floated free of hardware.
- **Note on the source.** The PDF is an OCR scan with noticeable character
  corruption (e.g. the opening line and several names are garbled). Quotations above
  are normalized against the known text and cited to printed page numbers; a cleaner
  edition should be pinned if this reading becomes load-bearing for a concept page.
