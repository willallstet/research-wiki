# The Reflex Machine and the Cybernetic Brain: The Critique of Abstraction and its Application to Computationalism

**By:** M. Chirimuuta (History & Philosophy of Science, University of Pittsburgh) ·
**Published:** *Perspectives on Science* 2020, vol. 28, no. 3, pp. 421–452 (MIT
Press)

A philosophy-of-science paper tracked for [[ai-and-machine-vision]] — specifically
the "brain is not a computer" debate and the limits of computational models of
cognition, which sit under [[will-allstetter]]'s **algorithmic sublime** interest
(awe and belief projected onto opaque systems). Read in full from the local PDF.

## What it argues

Chirimuuta reconstructs a **neglected third argument** against computationalism —
the *abstraction argument* — distinct from the two familiar ones (Searle's Chinese
Room / Block's Blockhead, and the Dreyfus-derived embodiment/embeddedness argument
of the "4E" program) (`c36e4ecca93df8135b44db351a4d6ca8.pdf:2`). Computationalism is
"the idea that the essence of cognition … is computation" (`…pdf:1`). The
abstraction argument is "a contribution to the philosophy of cognitive science,
rather than a claim … about the nature of cognition itself": scientific models "are
always generated via abstraction from the greater complexity of concrete systems in
nature," so a computational model of the brain "will be a much abbreviated
abstraction and will leave out the details of the organismic processes which are
actually responsible for sentience and flexible intelligence" (`…pdf:2`–3).

The key warning is against the **reification of abstractions** — treating a
predictively useful, simplified model as if it revealed "the true nature of the
objects and systems so depicted" (`…pdf:3`). Superficial functional equivalences
between animals and computers "should not be taken as evidence that the computer
model has captured something of the essence of organic intelligence" or that
scaling such models "will scale up to replicating the total cognitive capacities of
a living animal" (`…pdf:3`).

## The genealogy she reconstructs

The paper is arranged chronologically (`…pdf:5`):

- **The critique of abstraction, 1907–1938** — Bergson's rejection of the
  "spatialization of time," Whitehead's "fallacy of misplaced concreteness," and
  Husserl, plus the background of Gestalt psychology (`…pdf:3`).
- **Kurt Goldstein's organicism** — his *The Organism* (1934/1939) applies the
  critique of abstraction to biology. Organismic (holistic) biology holds that "in
  living organisms, unlike machines, there is an influence going from the direction
  of the whole to its constituent parts" (`…pdf:4`). Chirimuuta argues attention to
  the abstraction argument gives a "more charitable interpretation of organicism,"
  distinguishing it from metaphysical vitalism (`…pdf:4`).
- **The reflex machine** — before the brain-as-computer, the nervous system was
  imagined as "a reflex machine, an aggregate of discrete sensor and effector arcs"
  (T. H. Huxley compared it to a steam engine and a clock) (`…pdf:15`). Even
  Sherrington conceded "a simple reflex is probably a purely abstract conception …
  a convenient, if not a probable, fiction" (`…pdf:16`). Goldstein's objection is
  that treating the reflex as a real building block is the reifying error.
- **Canguilhem and Merleau-Ponty** — both read Goldstein closely; their criticisms
  of **cybernetics** (the era's AI) turn on the same point. Chirimuuta argues
  Merleau-Ponty is misread by the 4E tradition, which over-focuses on embodiment in
  *Phenomenology of Perception* and misses his critique of scientific abstraction.

## The core claim and its extension to AI

The reflex-machine arguments "can be transposed with little effort to make a case
against computationalism" (`…pdf:16`). Research programs — "whether they be
reflexology or cybernetics — that create the appearance of the descriptive and
explanatory adequacy of the machine-organism analogy do so by experimental
strictures that limit organismic behavior to over-simplified … operationalizations:
the abstract model achieves its success when the target of the model — the living
system — is itself put in a simplifying frame. This is the 'absolute artificialism'
of cybernetics" (`…pdf:32`). This "generalizes to other kinds of computational
models of brain processes": the computationalist "is too incautious" because "the
model's judicious neglect of biological complexity is likely to make it miss the
hard-to-duplicate features of organic cognition that we care most about —
sentience, flexibility, common sense, a sense of being in the world" (`…pdf:32`).
Just because computational abstraction is "useful for building specialist AI
programs … it should not be assumed that computation is the essence of cognition,
or that building AI expert systems is a step along the road to … general
intelligence" (`…pdf:32`).

## The forgotten politics

A closing section notes a **political background** via Canguilhem: slaves were
"animate machines for Aristotle — the original machines … humans marked out for
exploitation," and "Descartes does to the animal what Aristotle did to the slave:
he devalorizes it in order to justify its use by man as an instrument" (`…pdf:32`).
The mechanization of life and the technical utilization of the animal "are
inseparable." Chirimuuta suggests the 4E program may have "a forgotten politics,
just as … it depend[s] on a neglected philosophy of science" (`…pdf:33`).

## Assessment

- **A genuine contribution, well-executed.** Careful intellectual history that
  recovers a real and under-appreciated line of argument (abstraction, not
  embodiment) and traces it cleanly from Bergson/Whitehead through Goldstein to
  Canguilhem and Merleau-Ponty. The transposition to modern computationalism is
  argued rather than asserted.
- **Scope, honestly bounded.** It is a philosophy-of-science paper, not an empirical
  neuroscience result: it argues about what computational models *can license us to
  conclude*, and it explicitly flags the political-philosophy question (does
  Merleau-Ponty's anti-computationalism rest on a politics?) as future work.

# The Reflex Machine and the Cybernetic Brain

**By:** M. Chirimuuta (History & Philosophy of Science, University of Pittsburgh)
· **Published:** *Perspectives on Science* 28(3), 2020, pp. 421–457
(MIT Press, doi:10.1162/posc_a_00346) · read in full from the queued PDF.

A reading tracked for [[ai-and-machine-vision]] — a philosophy-of-science
argument about *why the brain is not a computer*, and about what an abstraction
leaves out when it models a living system as a machine.

## What it is

A scholarly reconstruction of a **neglected argument against computationalism**.
Chirimuuta distinguishes two anti-computationalist lines: the familiar
*embodiment / embeddedness* argument (the "4E" program — embodied, embedded,
ecological, enactive), and a second, older **abstraction argument** she wants to
recover and give primacy (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p2 @n/a`). Computationalism is
"the idea that the essence of cognition … is computation" (`The Reflex
Machine.pdf:p1 @n/a`); the abstraction argument is "a contribution to the
philosophy of cognitive science, rather than a claim … about the nature of
cognition itself" (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p2 @n/a`).

## Its central idea: the critique of abstraction

The core move: "scientific theories and models are always generated via
abstraction from the greater complexity of concrete systems," so a computational
model of the brain "will leave out the details of the organismic processes which
are actually responsible for sentience and flexible intelligence" — and a
"superficial appearance of equivalence of functions … should not be taken as
evidence that the computer model has captured something of the essence of organic
intelligence" (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p2–3 @n/a`).

She traces this **critique of abstraction** through early-20th-century philosophy
of science that the analytic canon forgot:

- **Bergson** on the spatialization of time — the intellect "is only the
  projection, necessarily on a plane, of a reality that possesses both relief and
  depth"; mechanistic biology gives only "partial views," and "with these partial
  views put end to end, you will not make even a beginning of the reconstruction
  of the whole" (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p9,p14 @n/a`).
- **Whitehead**'s **fallacy of misplaced concreteness** — "the error of mistaking
  the abstractions of science for the concrete things in the world that they are
  abstractions from"; philosophy is "the critic of abstractions" (`The Reflex
  Machine.pdf:p9 @n/a`).
- **Husserl**'s **Lifeworld** — the objective "true" world of science "is an
  abstraction that is actually not experienceable" (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p11
  @n/a`).

Applied to biology, this grounds **Goldstein**'s organicism: the "simple reflex"
is, in Sherrington's own words, "a purely abstract conception … a convenient, if
not a probable, fiction" (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p16 @n/a`), and Goldstein
rejects building a theory of the whole organism up from artificially isolated,
"atomistic" parts — "investigations under artificial conditions can never lead to
knowledge of natural performances" (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p17–18 @n/a`).
**Canguilhem** and **Merleau-Ponty** carry the argument to cybernetics: Canguilhem
warns that cybernetic feedback models create "a new class of objects" that
abstracts away the difference between organic and mechanical regulation, and that
"the magical aspect of simulation is strongly resistant to the exorcism of
science" (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p24 @n/a`); Merleau-Ponty names the "absolute
artificialism" of cybernetics a "nightmare, from which there is no awakening"
(`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p29 @n/a`), and offers the contemplation of painting as
the path back to the "brute, existent world."

There is also a **politics** in the tail: drawing on Canguilhem, Chirimuuta notes
that "slaves were animate machines for Aristotle," and that "Descartes does to the
animal what Aristotle did to the slave: he devalorizes it in order to justify its
use by man as an instrument" — the mechanization of life is "inseparable" from
"exploitable labor and resource" (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p33 @n/a`).

## Why it's here

- **Concept fit:** [[ai-and-machine-vision]] — this is the philosophical
  counter-weight to the wiki's AI-image and machine-vision material: a rigorous
  case that "solving intelligence" via ever-larger computational models rests on
  an under-examined abstraction. It complicates the technical optimism of
  [[flash-bon-inference-time-scaling]] and sharpens the cultural anxiety of
  [[new-models-taste-2026]] into a philosophy-of-science claim: the model's
  "judicious neglect of biological complexity is likely to make it miss the
  hard-to-duplicate features of organic cognition that we care most about"
  (`The Reflex Machine.pdf:p32 @n/a`).
- **Concept fit:** [[media-infrastructure]] — the closing turn to the *politics*
  of the machine-organism analogy (mechanism as inseparable from exploitable
  labor) rhymes with the wiki's interest in the material and economic substrate
  behind computation's mystique.
- **Contrast with [[ccru-writings-1997-2003]]:** where Ccru *embraces*
  computation as a quasi-magical, self-realizing force (the algorithmic sublime),
  Chirimuuta is the disenchanter — she calls out precisely the "magical aspect of
  simulation" as a philosophical error.

## Assessment

- **A rigorous secondary/philosophical source**, read on its own terms as an
  argument in the history and philosophy of science. Unlike the theory-fiction
  and art-theory readings in this wiki, it makes falsifiable-ish scholarly claims
  (about what Merleau-Ponty argued, about the lineage of the abstraction
  critique) and defends a thesis; its value here is as the strongest available
  articulation of the *case against* treating brains as computers.
- **High relevance, moderate difficulty.** Dense with Continental philosophy of
  science (Bergson, Whitehead, Husserl, Goldstein, Canguilhem, Merleau-Ponty),
  but the through-line — abstraction hides what matters — is exactly the critical
  lens [[will-allstetter]]'s AI work circles. A keystone for the "against the
  machine" side of [[ai-and-machine-vision]].
